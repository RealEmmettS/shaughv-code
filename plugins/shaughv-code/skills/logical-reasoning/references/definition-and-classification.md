# Definition & Classification

Much reasoning goes wrong before any inference is drawn — in unclear terms. A surprising
share of disputes are *verbal*: the parties use a word in different senses and argue past
each other. Pinning down meaning is reasoning hygiene.

## 1. Why definitions matter

Before evaluating an argument, check that key terms are used in one fixed sense (this is
also the antidote to equivocation; see `fallacies.md`). When two people "disagree," first
ask whether they'd still disagree once the central term is defined the same way. If not,
the dispute is merely verbal.

## 2. Kinds of definition (by purpose)

- **Lexical / reportive:** reports established usage (a dictionary definition). Can be
  *true or false* to actual usage.
- **Stipulative:** assigns a meaning by decree, often a new term or a special sense for a
  context ("for this paper, 'agent' means…"). Not true/false, but can be useful or not.
- **Precising:** sharpens a vague term for a purpose (defining "adult" as "18+" for a law).
  Reduces borderline cases.
- **Theoretical:** embeds a term in a theory ("temperature" as mean kinetic energy).
- **Persuasive:** smuggles an evaluation into the definition to win an argument by wording
  ("'taxation' means legalized theft"). A rhetorical move to flag, not a neutral definition.

## 3. Techniques (by method)

- **Genus and differentia:** the workhorse — name the broader class (genus) and the feature
  marking off the species ("a triangle is a *polygon* [genus] *with three sides*
  [differentia]").
- **Extensional / enumerative:** list members or examples ("a primary color is red, yellow,
  or blue"). Limited — can't list infinite or open classes.
- **Intensional:** give the defining properties (the connotation) rather than the members.
- **Ostensive:** point to instances ("*that* is the color crimson").
- **By synonym:** "to procrastinate is to delay." Quick but only as clear as the synonym.
- **Operational:** define by a measuring procedure ("harder = scratches the other").

## 4. Criteria for a good (lexical) definition

1. **State essential attributes** — prefer genus + differentia over accidental features.
2. **Not too broad or too narrow** — the *definiens* must pick out exactly the *definiendum*'s
   extension. "A bird is a feathered animal" (broad/narrow tests: is anything feathered not a
   bird? is any bird not feathered?).
3. **Not circular** — don't use the term (or an obvious cognate) in its own definition.
4. **Not negative when it can be affirmative** — say what a thing *is* ("a bachelor is an
   unmarried man," not "a bachelor is not married"); negatives are acceptable where the
   concept is itself privative ("orphan").
5. **Not figurative, obscure, or ambiguous** — no metaphor ("architecture is frozen music")
   or jargon more obscure than the term.

## 5. Classification and logical division

**Division** splits a class into subclasses; **classification** assigns things to classes.
Rules for a sound division:
- **One principle (fundamentum divisionis) at a time** — don't mix bases (dividing books by
  *both* subject and binding in one cut produces overlap).
- **Jointly exhaustive** — the subclasses together cover the whole class (no members left
  out).
- **Mutually exclusive** — no member falls into two subclasses (no overlap).
- **No jumping levels** — divide into immediate subclasses, not a mix of broad and narrow.

## 6. Critique checklist
Given a definition: Which kind/purpose is it? By what technique? Is it too broad, too
narrow, circular, negative-by-default, or figurative? Is it covertly persuasive? Given a
classification: single principle? exhaustive? exclusive?
