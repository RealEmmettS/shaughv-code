# Explanation & Inference to the Best Explanation

An **argument** gives reasons to believe *that* something is true. An **explanation** gives
an account of *why* something (already accepted) is the case. Confusing the two is a common
and consequential error — many "arguments" are really explanations, and judging them by the
wrong standard misfires.

## 1. Explanation vs. argument

- **Explanandum** — the thing being explained (the accepted fact).
- **Explanans** — what does the explaining (the proposed account).
"The pipe burst because the water froze and expanded" *explains* a burst pipe everyone
already grants; it isn't trying to prove the pipe burst. The tell: is the conclusion in
dispute (→ argument) or already accepted, with the passage saying why (→ explanation)?
Note the same sentence can serve either role depending on what's at issue.

## 2. Types of explanation

- **Causal** — cites the cause(s) that produced the event. The most common.
- **Functional / teleological** — explains by purpose or role ("the heart pumps blood *in
  order to* circulate oxygen").
- **Intentional** — explains action by an agent's reasons, beliefs, desires.
- **Reductive** — explains a phenomenon in terms of lower-level constituents (temperature →
  molecular motion).
- **Statistical** — explains by probabilistic regularities rather than deterministic cause.

## 3. Inference to the best explanation (abduction)

The reasoning pattern: a body of facts is observed; one hypothesis would, if true, explain
them better than any rival; therefore that hypothesis is probably true. This is how
diagnosis, detective work, and much of science proceed. It is *inductive* — the conclusion
is probable, not guaranteed — and only as good as (a) the field of hypotheses considered and
(b) the criteria used to rank them. Its characteristic failure is a **thin hypothesis set**:
picking the "best" of two when the true explanation was never on the list.

## 4. Criteria for a good explanation

When ranking competing explanations, score on:
- **Explanatory scope** — how much of the data it accounts for. More is better.
- **Explanatory power / depth** — how *well* (not just how much) it accounts for them; does
  it make the data expected rather than merely compatible?
- **Testability / falsifiability** — does it yield checkable predictions? An explanation that
  forbids nothing explains nothing.
- **Simplicity / parsimony** — fewer assumptions and entities, all else equal (Occam).
- **Conservatism / coherence** — fit with well-established background knowledge; overturning
  a lot demands strong warrant.
- **Fruitfulness** — does it suggest new lines of inquiry or successful novel predictions?
No single criterion is decisive; weigh them together, and watch for trade-offs (a simpler
theory with less scope vs. a complex one explaining more).

## 5. The hypothetico-deductive pattern

Scientific reasoning often runs: form a hypothesis → deduce an observable prediction → test
→ revise. A passed test *confirms* (raises probability) but doesn't prove (affirming the
consequent is invalid — see `fallacies.md`); a failed prediction *disconfirms* via modus
tollens, though auxiliary assumptions can absorb the blow (Duhem's point). Strong tests are
**risky** ones — predictions likely to fail if the hypothesis is wrong.

## 6. Pseudo-explanations (what to flag)

- **Vacuous / circular** — restates the explanandum in fancier words ("opium induces sleep
  because of its dormitive virtue").
- **Ad hoc** — patched after the fact solely to save the hypothesis, with no independent
  support or testable consequence.
- **Unfalsifiable** — compatible with *any* possible observation; explains everything,
  predicts nothing.
- **Just-so story** — a plausible-sounding causal/functional tale with no independent
  evidence it actually obtains.

## 7. Worked best-explanation comparison

*Data:* the lawn is wet this morning; the sky was clear all night; the sprinkler timer is
set for 5 a.m.
- H1: it rained — *scope* covers wet lawn but conflicts with "clear all night"
  (low conservatism with the observation).
- H2: the sprinkler ran — covers the wet lawn, fits the timer, consistent with clear skies;
  testable (check the timer log, neighbors' dry lawns).
H2 wins on scope-fit + testability + conservatism. The verdict stays provisional: a leaking
pipe (an unlisted H3) would beat both — hence always ask whether the hypothesis set is
complete.
