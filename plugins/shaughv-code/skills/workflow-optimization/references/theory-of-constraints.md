# Theory of Constraints (TOC)

**Lens question this theory answers:** *What single factor most limits this
workflow — and is all effort focused on it?*

---

## Origin and core philosophy

The Theory of Constraints is a management paradigm introduced by Eliyahu M.
Goldratt in his 1984 business novel *The Goal*, and extended to project management
in *Critical Chain* (1997).

Its central claim: **any system is limited in reaching its goal by a very small
number of constraints — usually just one.** TOC adopts the idiom "a chain is no
stronger than its weakest link." Improving any link *other* than the weakest
produces no gain in overall system output. Therefore, find the weakest link, and
focus there.

The argument is simple: if nothing limited a system, its output would be infinite,
which is impossible. So a constraint always exists. Only by increasing flow
*through the constraint* can the whole system's output rise.

TOC measures a system with three numbers: **throughput** (the rate at which the
system generates its goal — e.g. money through sales), **inventory** (everything
the system has invested in things it intends to process), and **operating
expense** (what it spends to turn inventory into throughput). Improvements should
raise throughput and/or lower inventory and operating expense.

## The Five Focusing Steps

This is TOC's core procedure — a process of ongoing improvement (POOGI):

1. **Identify** the system's constraint — the one factor most limiting the goal.
2. **Exploit** the constraint — get the most out of it *as it is*, before
   spending money. Ensure it is never idle, never starved, never working on the
   wrong thing.
3. **Subordinate** everything else to the above decision — every other step
   should run at the pace of, and in service of, the constraint. Non-constraints
   should *not* be maximized; that just creates inventory.
4. **Elevate** the constraint — if it is still the limit after exploitation,
   invest to increase its capacity (more equipment, more people, etc.).
5. **Repeat** — once a constraint is "broken" (no longer the limit), a new one
   emerges. Go back to step 1. **Warning:** do not let *inertia* — old rules and
   habits built around the old constraint — become the new constraint.

## Key concepts

- **Constraint types** — a constraint is usually **equipment** (capacity limits
  output), **people** (a skills shortage, or limiting mental models), or
  **policy** (a written or unwritten rule that caps output). Policy constraints
  are extremely common and often the cheapest to fix.
- **Internal vs. external constraint** — *internal:* demand exceeds what the
  workflow can deliver (focus on opening up the constraint). *External:* the
  workflow can do more than demand requires (focus on creating more demand).
- **A constraint is not a breakdown.** A machine failing is a breakdown. The
  constraint is the limiting factor that caps output *even when nothing goes
  wrong*.
- **Buffers** — protective time or work placed *before* the constraint so it is
  never starved, and *after* it so downstream problems never block it. Buffers
  absorb the normal variation of the rest of the system.
- **Drum-Buffer-Rope (DBR)** — the constraint is the *drum* setting the pace; the
  *buffer* protects it; the *rope* releases new work into the system only as fast
  as the constraint consumes it (preventing pile-up).
- **Buffer management** — a visual green/yellow/red system showing how much buffer
  remains, so the whole system can see and respond to the constraint's needs.
- **VATI analysis** — classifies the *shape* of a workflow's flow: **V** (one
  input diverges to many outputs — risk: "robbing" of shared materials), **A**
  (many inputs converge to one output — risk: synchronization), **T** (shared
  components combine many ways — both risks), **I** (a straight sequence — the
  constraint is simply the slowest step). For non-physical workflows, draw the
  flow of work or information and the same V/A/T/I shapes appear.

## How TOC identifies and prioritizes improvement

- **Identify:** Find where work piles up, which step has the least spare capacity,
  and which step would halt the whole workflow soonest if it stopped. (Checklist
  B.) Determine whether the constraint is equipment, people, or policy.
- **Prioritize:** This is TOC's distinctive contribution — **the constraint
  dictates priority, absolutely.** Work on the constraint first; everything else
  waits. An improvement to a non-constraint is, by definition, not worth doing
  yet. "Every bottleneck avoided beats every bottleneck resolved."

## Applying the TOC lens in the Sweep

For the workflow under review, ask (run Checklist B alongside):

1. Where does work visibly pile up? What is the single biggest limiting step?
2. Is that constraint equipment, a person/skill, or a policy?
3. Is it internal (can't meet demand) or external (demand is the limit)?
4. Is the constraint being fully exploited — never starved, never idle, never
   working the wrong item?
5. Are the non-constraint steps subordinated to it, or are they being locally
   maximized and creating queues?
6. Is there constraint *inertia* — rules or habits left over from a constraint
   that has already been broken?

Record the constraint and its handling in the Lens Ledger.

## Important note for the neutral shortlist

TOC is inherently *decisive* — it says "fix the constraint, period." When you
reach the Converge step, present TOC's "constraint-first" logic as **one lens's
strong recommendation**, clearly attributed to TOC — not as the skill overriding
the user's own prioritization. The user still owns the final ranking call. TOC's
verdict is powerful input, not a substitute for the user's judgment.
