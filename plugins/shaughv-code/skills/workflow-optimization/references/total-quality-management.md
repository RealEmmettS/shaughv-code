# Total Quality Management (TQM)

**Lens question this theory answers:** *Does this workflow have a healthy
quality system — is improvement everyone's job, customer-driven, and continuous?*

---

## Origin and core philosophy

Total Quality Management is an organization-wide approach to embedding a permanent
climate of continuous improvement, so that everyone continually improves their
ability to deliver products and services the customer finds valuable.

It emerged in the late 1970s–80s as Western firms responded to Japanese
manufacturing quality. It draws on the teachings of W. Edwards Deming, Joseph
Juran, Armand Feigenbaum, Kaoru Ishikawa, and Philip Crosby. The U.S. Navy coined
the specific term "Total Quality Management" around 1985. TQM peaked as a named
movement around 1993 and was later largely absorbed into ISO 9000, Lean, and Six
Sigma — but its philosophy underpins all of them, and that philosophy is what this
lens checks.

The core idea: quality is not the job of an inspection department at the end of
the line. It is **everyone's responsibility, in every department** — sales,
finance, design, engineering, support, not just production.

## The key TQM concepts (the detection lens)

The U.S. Navy's foundational TQM effort distilled four ideas — use these as the
lens:

1. **Quality is defined by the customer's requirements.** Not by internal
   standards, not by the people running the process.
2. **Top management has direct responsibility for quality improvement.** It cannot
   be delegated away or left to chance; it needs funding, training, staffing, and
   goal-setting from the top.
3. **Quality comes from systematic analysis and improvement of work processes** —
   improving the *process*, not exhorting individuals to try harder.
4. **Quality improvement is continuous and organization-wide.** It never stops,
   and it involves everyone.

## Signature tools and structures

- **PDCA cycle** (Plan-Do-Check-Act, also Plan-Do-Study-Act) — the engine of
  continuous improvement: plan a change, do it on a small scale, check the
  results, act to standardize or adjust, then repeat. (DMAIC in Six Sigma is a
  descendant of PDCA.)
- **The Seven Basic Tools of Quality** — check sheet, histogram, Pareto chart,
  fishbone/cause-and-effect diagram, control chart, scatter diagram, and flowchart
  /stratification. A standard, accessible analysis kit.
- **Quality circles** — small groups of workers who meet regularly to identify and
  solve quality problems in their own area.
- **Cross-functional teams** — both ad-hoc teams for immediate process issues and
  standing teams for long-term process improvement.
- **Baldrige Criteria** — the Malcolm Baldrige National Quality Award judges
  organizations on seven categories: Leadership, Strategy, Customers, Measurement
  & analysis & knowledge management, Workforce, Operations, and Results. Juran
  considered these the most widely accepted description of what TQM entails — they
  make a useful audit frame.

## How TQM identifies and prioritizes improvement

- **Identify:** Examine whether the workflow has the *system* for quality —
  customer-defined standards, a feedback loop, systematic (not ad-hoc) problem
  analysis, a continuous-improvement cadence, and management ownership. Gaps in
  that system are the findings.
- **Prioritize:** TQM is less prescriptive about ranking than TOC or Six Sigma.
  It directs effort by customer impact and by management's strategic goals (cost,
  quality, schedule, customer satisfaction), and favors continuous incremental
  improvement via PDCA over one-time fixes.

## Applying the TQM lens in the Sweep

For the workflow under review, ask (run Checklist E alongside):

1. Is quality built into every step, or quarantined to an inspection/QA step at
   the end? (Inspecting quality in at the end is a TQM anti-pattern.)
2. Has the *customer* of this workflow defined what "good" means — and is there a
   feedback loop carrying their input back into the process?
3. Is there a built-in continuous-improvement cadence (a PDCA loop), or does the
   workflow only change when something breaks?
4. Are the people doing the work empowered and expected to flag and fix problems?
5. When problems occur, are they analyzed systematically, or just worked around
   individually and forgotten?
6. Does improvement of this workflow have real management ownership and resources,
   or is it nobody's actual job?

Record gaps in the quality system in the Lens Ledger.

## Relationship to the other lenses

TQM overlaps heavily with Lean (continuous improvement, customer value) and Six
Sigma (systematic analysis, the Pareto/fishbone tools). That overlap is expected
and fine. TQM's *distinct* contribution to the Sweep is the **systemic and
cultural** view: it is less about finding a specific waste or defect and more
about whether the workflow has the organizational machinery to keep finding and
fixing problems on its own. Use this lens to catch the absence of that machinery —
a workflow can have no visible defects today and still fail the TQM lens because
it has no way to stay good.
