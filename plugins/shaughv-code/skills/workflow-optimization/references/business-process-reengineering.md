# Business Process Re-engineering (BPR)

**Lens question this theory answers:** *Should this workflow be improved — or
fundamentally redesigned from scratch?*

---

## Origin and core philosophy

Business Process Re-engineering is a management strategy pioneered in the early
1990s, most associated with Michael Hammer's 1990 *Harvard Business Review*
article "Reengineering Work: Don't Automate, Obliterate" and the book
*Reengineering the Corporation* by Hammer and James Champy. By 1993, around 60% of
Fortune 500 companies had launched or planned re-engineering efforts.

The canonical definition: **the fundamental rethinking and radical redesign of
business processes to achieve dramatic improvements in critical measures of
performance — cost, quality, service, and speed.**

What makes BPR distinct from the other theories is right there in the words
*fundamental* and *radical*. Where Lean, TQM, and Six Sigma improve a process
incrementally, BPR challenges whether the process should exist in its current form
*at all*. Hammer's core accusation: most work being done does not add value for
the customer, and that work should be **eliminated, not accelerated**. Automating
a wasteful process just produces faster waste.

BPR insists on a **holistic, whole-process** view. Optimizing individual
sub-processes — each owned by a different functional silo, with no one accountable
for the end-to-end outcome — cannot fix a process that is fundamentally outmoded.
You have to redesign the whole thing.

## How BPR works

Re-engineering starts not with the process but with first principles:

1. **Revisit mission and goals.** "Does our mission need to be redefined? Are our
   strategic goals aligned with it? Who are our customers, and what do they
   actually need?" Organizations often run on questionable assumptions about
   customer needs.
2. **Then redesign the process** to serve those goals — identify, analyze, and
   re-design the core business processes for dramatic gains in cost, quality,
   service, and speed.

BPR works across four dimensions, with **strategy as the primary driver**:
**organization** (structure, units, distribution of work), **technology**,
**strategy**, and **people** (training, motivation, rewards). A process view is
the common framework tying them together.

Technology is treated as an **enabler of new ways of working** — not as a way to
speed up the old ways. The early BPR literature listed "disruptive technologies"
that should make people rethink work: shared databases (information available
everywhere at once), expert systems (generalists doing specialist work),
telecommunication networks, decision-support tools, automatic identification and
tracking, and so on.

Note: BPR's modern successor is **Business Process Management (BPM)** — the same
process-centric drive, applied in a less radical, more continuous way.

## The BPR process-analysis checklist

When examining a process for re-engineering, BPR practitioners look to:

- **Reduce hand-offs** — each hand-off between people, teams, or systems is a
  delay and an error risk.
- **Centralize data** — make information available where it is needed instead of
  fragmented across silos and shadow databases.
- **Reduce delays** — cut the waiting and queuing between steps.
- **Free resources faster.**
- **Combine similar activities** — consolidate work currently fragmented across
  roles.
- **Remove non-value-adding tasks** — eliminate, don't streamline.

A central BPR practice is building a **large-scale "panoramic" process map** that
captures every activity, hand-off, and information flow across departmental
boundaries. Such maps make visible the hidden dependencies, shadow databases, and
rework loops that senior management never sees. (See `diagramming.md`.)

## How BPR identifies and prioritizes improvement

- **Identify:** Map the whole end-to-end process. Look for fragmentation across
  silos, excessive hand-offs, non-value-adding work, and steps that exist only
  because of outdated assumptions. (Checklist D.)
- **Prioritize:** The big BPR question is the **radical-vs-incremental fork**.
  BPR's prioritization is binary at the top level: is this process fundamentally
  outmoded (then a clean-sheet redesign beats any amount of tuning) or basically
  sound (then incremental methods — Lean, Six Sigma, TQM — are the right tools)?

## Applying the BPR lens in the Sweep

For the workflow under review, ask (run Checklist D alongside):

1. If you designed this workflow today, from a blank sheet, knowing current
   technology and customer needs — would it look anything like it does now?
2. How many hand-offs does it contain? Is anyone accountable for the *whole*
   end-to-end outcome, or only for fragments?
3. Which steps exist only because of an old rule, an old constraint, or an old
   technology limit that no longer applies?
4. Is data fragmented across silos, re-keyed, or held in shadow systems?
5. Are there steps the customer would not pay for and would not miss?
6. **The verdict:** is this workflow fundamentally outmoded (recommend radical
   redesign) or basically sound but inefficient (recommend incremental
   improvement)?

Record the fragmentation/hand-off findings *and* the radical-vs-incremental
verdict in the Lens Ledger.

## Watch-outs

- BPR was widely criticized as a cover for downsizing and as "Taylorism
  rebranded," and many re-engineering efforts failed on the people dimension —
  resistance to change. If the lens recommends radical redesign, also flag the
  change-management and human cost; a redesign that ignores the people who do the
  work tends to fail on the drawing board's promises.
- "Radical redesign" is a strong recommendation. Present it as the BPR lens's
  finding, with its reasoning visible — the user owns the decision to take that
  path.
