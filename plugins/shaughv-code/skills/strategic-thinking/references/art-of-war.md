# The Art of War — the Positioning Lens

*Sun Tzu's classic reads, in modern terms, as a theory of position: win the
contest before it starts by being on the right ground, against the right opponent,
at the right time — or don't fight it at all. This lens asks the macro questions:
should you engage, where, and how do you make the outcome decided in advance?*

This file distills the work's principles in plain terms (the text itself is an
ancient public-domain classic; what follows is an original synthesis, not a
translation). Apply it by asking the **detection questions** against the user's
real situation — don't recite the principles at them.

## When this lens does the most work

- The user hasn't yet decided *whether* to engage, or *where*.
- They feel outmatched, or they're about to attack the opponent's strength head-on.
- The contest is costly and a long, grinding fight would hurt even the winner.
- Big, structural, slow-moving situations (markets, careers, rivalries) more than
  fast tactical exchanges (those lean on the Five Rings lens).

## Principle 1 — Assess across the five fundamentals

Before anything, compare yourself and the other side across five dimensions. The
side that is stronger across these wins before the first move.

- **The Way** — legitimacy, morale, alignment. Are your people (or you) genuinely
  committed to this objective? Is the cause one that holds under pressure? A
  divided or half-hearted side loses to a unified one of equal strength.
- **Timing / conditions** — the moment, the season, what's changing in the
  environment. Is now the time, or is the situation still ripening against you?
- **Ground** — the terrain you'll contest on: the rules, the venue, the structural
  conditions you can't change but must use.
- **Leadership** — judgment, credibility, decisiveness, the trust of those you lead.
- **Organization / method** — your discipline, logistics, resources, and how
  reliably you can execute.

**Detection questions:**
- Honestly, on each of the five, are you stronger or weaker than the other side?
- Which fundamental is your biggest edge — and are you actually using it?
- Which is your biggest exposure — and can you shore it up before engaging?

## Principle 2 — Know yourself and know the other side

The famous core: know both, and you are not endangered across a hundred contests;
know yourself but not them (or them but not yourself), and you win some and lose
some; know neither, and you're in danger every time.

**Detection questions:**
- How well do you actually understand the other side's objective — not what they
  say, but what they're really trying to achieve?
- Where are you guessing about them, and how decisive is that guess to your plan?
- Do you know your own real limits, or the version of yourself you'd like to be?

## Principle 3 — Subdue without fighting (the supreme skill)

The highest achievement isn't winning a hundred battles — it's achieving the
objective without the battle. Best is to thwart the opponent's plan or strategy;
next, to break up their alliances and support; next, to confront their forces
directly; worst is the long, costly, grinding assault on their strongest point.

**Detection questions:**
- Is there a way to get your objective such that the contest simply never happens?
- Can you make the other side's plan unworkable rather than out-fighting them?
- Can you remove their support, their allies, or their reason to oppose you?
- If you've jumped straight to "how do I beat them head-on," back up a level.

## Principle 4 — Win first, then fight

The disciplined strategist arranges things so that victory is already decided, then
engages to collect it. The reckless one engages first and hopes to find victory in
the fight. Position, prepare, and shape the situation until the outcome is settled
before you commit.

**Detection questions:**
- What would have to be true for this to be already won before you engage?
- Are you committing in hope of winning, or because you've made winning likely?
- What can you do *now*, before any confrontation, that decides the outcome?

## Principle 5 — Avoid strength, strike weakness (be like water)

Don't throw yourself at the opponent's strongest point. Flow around it, like water
finding the low ground — strike where they're thin, unprepared, or can't easily
defend. Concentrate your force where they're dispersed; appear where you're not
expected.

**Detection questions:**
- Where is the other side strongest — and are you attacking *there* by default?
- Where are they thin, slow, distracted, or overcommitted?
- Can you concentrate everything you have at one decisive point while they're spread?

## Principle 6 — Shape perception (with the ethics guard)

Sun Tzu treats the management of the opponent's perception as central: be
unpredictable, don't telegraph your real intent, let them prepare for the wrong
thing. **This is exactly where the Ethics & Proportionality guard applies.** In
legitimate contexts — competition, negotiation, sport, games — controlling what you
reveal and when is fair and smart. In contexts that demand good faith, or where the
other party relies on you, deception becomes manipulation or fraud. Keep it to
*what you're entitled to control* (your own signals, timing, and disclosures), not
to lies that harm someone who trusts you.

**Detection questions:**
- What are you signaling, intentionally or not, and what does it let them prepare for?
- What can you legitimately keep ambiguous to preserve your options?
- Does any move here depend on someone trusting you in order to be harmed? If so,
  it's out — find another line.

## Principle 7 — The economy of force; prolonged conflict benefits no one

Even a winning war drains the winner. Speed and decisiveness beat drawn-out
struggle; no one profits from a contest that grinds on. Factor the *cost* of the
fight — money, time, attention, relationships, reputation — into whether and how to
engage, not just the odds of winning.

**Detection questions:**
- What does this contest cost you even if you win it? Is the objective worth that?
- Is there a faster, more decisive version that ends it before it grinds?
- Are you about to win the battle and lose the war (the relationship, the next
  round, your standing)?

## Recording the findings

In the Lens Ledger, capture the *positioning* read: Should they engage at all? On
what ground? Where's the opponent's weakness and the user's edge? What would make
victory decided in advance? What's the cost, and is the objective worth it? Note
where the read of the other side is assumption rather than fact.
