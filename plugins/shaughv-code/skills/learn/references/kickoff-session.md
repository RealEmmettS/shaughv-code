# Kickoff Session

For new topics where the user hasn't started yet. The output of a Kickoff session is:

1. A clear, refined **learning goal**
2. A first-pass **syllabus** (a library of chunks organized by importance and order)
3. A **Learning Journal** scaffold for future sessions

Kickoff sessions usually take 20–40 minutes. Don't rush them — the time invested here
saves much more time later by preventing aimless practice.

A Kickoff has two phases run in sequence:

- **Phase 1: Goal Setting** (10 steps)
- **Phase 2: Meta-Learning Research** (5 steps)

If the user has limited time, Phase 1 alone is a valid session — Phase 2 can happen later.

---

## Phase 1: Goal Setting

The 10-step procedure for crafting a learning goal that's clear, specific, and
achievable.

### Step 1 — What's Your Broad Aspiration?

**1.1.** Write down what brought you to this idea. Be as vague as you want and capture
the highest-level ideas related to this goal.

**1.2.** Connect this broad hope to your passions and interests for increased motivation.

**1.3.** Why are you even creating a goal in the first place? What caused you to start?

> Capture this in the journal under the **Goal — Aspiration** section. The aspiration
> can be vague; later steps will sharpen it.

### Step 2 — Specific: Provide More Detail to Your Goal

**2.1.** Focus on one skill at a time. Concentrate your energy on a single skill to
avoid diluting your effort.

**2.2.** What are the specific outcomes you hope to achieve?

**2.3.** Paint a picture of what it will look like when you successfully achieve this
goal.

**2.4.** How are you going to achieve this?

**2.5.** Choose a lovable project that excites you. Select a skill that you genuinely
enjoy or look forward to learning.

**2.6. What are you specifically NOT going to cover in this project?**
- Create a list of topics and sub-skills you will cover and a list of topics and
  sub-skills you will NOT cover.

> 2.6 is the most underused sub-question. Naming what you're *not* doing is what makes
> the scope tractable. Push hard here.

**2.7.** Does your goal clearly and specifically state what you are trying to achieve?

**2.8.** If your goal is particularly large or lofty, try breaking it down into smaller
goals.

**2.9. Use project-based learning.** Create tangible outcomes that keep you motivated
and engaged.
- Example: *"If learning video editing, aim to create a 3-minute short film."*

### Step 3 — How Good Do You Need To Be?

**3.1.** What is your target performance level with this goal?
- Reference `references/proficiency-levels.md` — pick a level (Aware, Novice, Amateur,
  Intermediate, Proficient, Competitive, Expert).

**3.2.** Is this a goal that requires you to be the best, or can you achieve your goal
at an intermediate or novice level?

**3.3. What is the lowest level of performance you would be happy with achieving?**
- It is often faster to set a lower performance goal.

**3.4.** If you are looking to enjoy the pursuit of a new skill or new knowledge, then
the performance level you are looking for is when practicing is enjoyable.

**3.5.** Talk to people who have already achieved what you want to achieve. Do some very
light research online for people at the level you would like to be. Watch YouTube
videos from the people who have achieved what you want to achieve.

> The "Jack of all trades" principle: in the time it takes to reach world-class in one
> skill, you could reach intermediate in many. Be intentional about where world-class
> is actually needed.

### Step 4 — Measure: How Will You Measure Progress?

**4.1.** What are you going to use to measure your success?
- Example: *"I will know I have reached my goal when I can walk through the 5 most
  popular technical interview questions on paper."*

**4.2.** How will you know when you have reached your goal? This could take many forms:
passing a standardized test, having a specific hit rate, having a minimum number of
misses, etc.

**4.3.** How will I prove to myself that I know what I hope to learn?

**4.4.** To stay motivated, set weekly goals and break them into five to ten small,
manageable daily tasks.

### Step 5 — Connect To Your Values

**5.1.** Why is this goal important to you?

**5.2.** What are the values associated with this goal? How does pursuing this goal
align with these values?

**5.3.** How does your identity play a role in setting and pursuing this goal? Do you
see yourself as someone who pursues this type of goal, and if not, why?

**5.4. Determine if this is an instrumental or intrinsic goal.**
- Instrumental — the goal is a means to a broader result
- Intrinsic — the goal is pursued for its own sake

> The instrumental/intrinsic distinction matters a lot. Instrumental goals can be
> redirected if the broader result becomes achievable another way. Intrinsic goals
> can't.

### Step 6 — Relevant: Connect It To Real-World Use Cases

**6.1.** How does my goal align with my broad aspirations and values?

**6.2.** Does my practice improve my ability to perform in the real-world scenario I
would like to improve at?

**6.3. How can I adjust my practice to be similar to the conditions I will face in the
real world?**
- Higher level of stress?
- Same materials?
- Same or similar location?

**6.4. Stare at who you want to become.** Observe top performers in your field and
envision yourself in their position to boost motivation.

### Step 7 — Time-Bound: Create A Realistic Timeline

**7.1.** How much time do I have to dedicate to this goal each week?

**7.2.** When do I want to reach this goal?

**7.3.** How many hours, roughly, do I estimate this project to take?

**7.4.** Ensure that your deadline includes the inevitable variability of your schedule.

**7.5. Imagine that you've completed your goal.** Work backward and write down the
steps that it took to get there.

**7.6.** Goals with deadlines further than three months out can be demotivating. Create
smaller stepping stones that can be reached within three months. Keep in mind that it
takes about 8 weeks (or 20 hours of dedicated practice) for new skills to develop.

### Step 8 — Achievable: Set Proper Expectations

**8.1.** Is the skill I am trying to learn too advanced?

**8.2.** Do I have the necessary foundation to tackle this new topic?

**8.3.** Do I really have the time to dedicate to this goal?

**8.4.** What happens when I miss a session or multiple sessions?

**8.5.** Does my plan account for me getting a proper amount of sleep each night?

### Step 9 — Adjust Your Goal

**9.1.** If you do not currently have the time to dedicate to your goal, adjust it to
fit your available time or make more time.

**9.2.** If your goal's scope is too large, break it down and remove unnecessary parts
to reach your very specific goal.

**9.3. Double the length of your learning project or halve the scope or performance
level.** We are often poor judges of the time or complexity of topics until it is too
late. After completing multiple projects, you'll have a better sense of how much you
can take on.

> The doubling/halving rule is hard to apply but consistently right. Resist the urge to
> believe *this* time will be different.

### Step 10 — Compile Your Goal

**10.1. Reframe your goal in the present tense and use positive language.**
- Don't say *"My goal is to not fail the German placement test."*
- Say *"My goal is to get a B1 on the German placement test."*

**10.2.** What is the impact of achieving the goal? How will achieving this goal impact
your life or the lives of others?

**10.3. Implementation Intentions.** Create a specific plan for when, where, and how
you will practice for your goal.
- Example: *"I will do at least [number of minutes] of [activity] on [day] at [time of
  day] at/in [place]."*
- Example: *"I will [verb] for [quantity of time] after/before [existing routine
  habit]."*

**10.4. Rewrite your goal using the above information** and place it in a visible area
that will remind you to stick with it.
- Example: *"In the next three months, I aim to consistently run 3K three times a
  week, improving my pace to reach a 6-minute kilometer for at least 1K of that
  distance."*

> The compiled goal goes at the top of the journal. It's the reference point for every
> Session and Review afterward.

## Phase 2: Meta-Learning Research

Once the goal is set, conduct preliminary research to build a syllabus before practicing.

### Step 1 — How Long to Research?

**1.1.** Calculate how long you are planning to spend learning this subject by using
your deadline. If you don't have a deadline, estimate based on your goals.

**1.2.** Before you start practicing or studying, **spend about 10% of that time
researching** the overall context, structure, and scope of the learning project.

**1.3. Set checkpoints** to check in with your learning methods and your understanding
of the context. For every 4.5 hours of practice, evaluate your learning methods for
about 30 minutes.

**1.4. Don't get stuck in "Tutorial Hell."** Research is to make practice more
effective, not to replace it.

**1.5.** If you are doing more research after you have already started, focus on:
- 1.5.1. What part has been the most difficult for you?
- 1.5.2. Are your learning activities aligned with your goal?

> For wicked-environment topics (slow/noisy feedback), increase the research budget to
> 15–20%. See SKILL.md's Wicked-Environment Guard.

### Step 2 — Identify the Five Chunk Types

While researching, watch for and capture five types of chunks. See
`references/chunk-types.md` for full detail and examples.

**2.1. Confusion signals** — new ideas you don't understand yet. Record anything
challenging at first glance. Dig into your confusion and break it down.

**2.2. Facts** — atomic pieces of information that can be proved true.

**2.3. Concepts** — abstract ideas that you need to be aware of, able to explain, and
applied to various contexts.

**2.4. Procedures** — actions or series of actions that must be rehearsed and honed.

**2.5. Methods** — how skills are practiced or subjects are studied.

### Step 3 — Researching

**3.1.** Dedicate a section of your journal to recording the Facts, Concepts,
Procedures, and Methods (and Confusion signals) as you come across them. Just write a
few words covering the main idea — depth comes later.

**3.2. Find at least 10 sources** covering the subject or skill. Determine quality by
skimming for step-by-step examples and helpful considerations. Record any new chunks.
**Narrow down to the top 3 sources** that fit your goal and depth.

**3.3. Talk to and/or observe experts.** Most niches have experts with blogs or YouTube
channels who share opinions, experiences, and recommendations for beginners. Look for
sources that talk about beginners' mistakes or recap learning journeys — these can save
hours of trial-and-error.

### Step 4 — Creating a Syllabus

Now you have a library of chunks. Build a syllabus by focusing on three things:

1. **What is important?**
2. **When is it important?**
3. **How does it connect to everything else?**

**4.1. Review your goal and consider what failure would look like.** What are the key
mistakes you should avoid? Which chunks help you prevent failure or are essential to
your safety/performance?

**4.2.** Which chunks determine how fast or how well you can perform the skill?

**4.3. Which chunks are built on the understanding of other chunks?** Looking at
curriculums, tables of contents, and syllabuses can help you see how teachers organize
material.

**4.4. Create a concept map** to better understand the higher-level connections between
chunks of the skill or subject.

**4.5. In what order are you going to tackle these chunks?** Often a few facts and
concepts are paired with one or more procedures.

> The syllabus goes in the journal under **Syllabus / Chunk Library**. It's not fixed
> — it evolves as new chunks surface during practice.

### Step 5 — Preparing to Practice or Study

**5.1.** What are the things you need to get started? Equipment, a place to practice,
a coach or teacher, time to dedicate?

> This step transitions from Kickoff to first Session. The journal scaffold should be
> complete: Goal, Syllabus, Chunk Library, and the question *"What chunk will I work
> on first?"*

---

## Closing the Kickoff session

Before ending:

1. **Confirm the journal is complete enough to use.** Goal compiled, Syllabus drafted,
   Chunk Library populated with at least the first set of chunks identified.
2. **Schedule the first Session.** When will the user actually start practicing? What
   chunk will they choose first?
3. **Set a Review date.** Weekly review is the default cadence — pick the day.
4. **Present the journal** as a markdown artifact in the conversation. The user owns
   it from here.

## Facilitation notes for Kickoff sessions

- **Don't skip Goal Setting Step 2.6** ("what are you NOT going to cover"). It's the
  scope-control step.
- **Don't skip Step 3** ("how good do you need to be"). Most people pursue Expert when
  Intermediate is what they actually want.
- **Step 9.3 (double/halve)** feels overcautious. Apply it anyway.
- **Step 10.3 (Implementation Intentions)** — the specific plan for when/where/how —
  is the most predictive component of whether the user actually starts. Don't let them
  finish without it.
- **For Phase 2, push for at least 10 sources** even if it feels like overkill. The
  point is breadth-of-survey, not depth-of-reading.
