# Proficiency Levels

The 8-level framework for identifying where the user stands in their learning journey
for a topic. Used in pre-flight diagnostics, Goal Setting Step 3 (target performance
level), and Course-Correct sessions.

## Why this matters

Methods that work at one level often fail at another. A Novice needs examples and
repetition; an Expert needs unfamiliar challenges and peer feedback. Matching the
methods to the level prevents both **information overload** (Novice methods at Expert
level: too thin, frustrating) and **cognitive overload** (Expert methods at Novice
level: too dense, overwhelming).

Two cognitive biases shape this:

- **Cognitive Load Theory** — there's a real limit on how much new information the
  brain can process. Match information density to current capacity.
- **Dunning-Kruger Effect** — confidence and competence track each other unevenly.
  Confidence dips at predictable points (Amateur → Intermediate, and Proficient →
  Competitive). Frustration at those points is structural, not a signal to quit.

## The S-curve and the Dunning-Kruger curve

Progress follows an S-curve: slow at the start, rapid in the middle, tapering at high
levels. Confidence follows a different shape — rising quickly through Amateur, dipping
sharply at Intermediate as the user realizes how much they don't know, recovering
through Proficient, dipping again at Competitive (where perfectionism kicks in), and
stabilizing at Expert.

Keep both curves in mind during facilitation.

## The skill ceiling and domain breadth

Two factors shape every learning curve:

- **Skill ceiling** — how high proficiency can go in a given area. Music has an
  effectively infinite ceiling; basic bike-riding has a defined one.
- **Domain breadth** — how many sub-skills the topic contains. Graphic design includes
  color theory, typography, layout, software tools — many sub-skills.

For broad domains, the user's level varies *by sub-skill*. The user might be Proficient
in color theory but Novice in typography. Be specific.

## The "jack of all trades" principle

In the time it takes to reach world-class in one skill, you could reach Intermediate in
many or Proficient in several. Be intentional about where world-class is actually
needed. Often Intermediate is the goal that matches reality.

This belongs in Goal Setting Step 3.3 — *"What is the lowest level of performance you
would be happy with achieving?"*

## Quick diagnostic

For pre-flight, three questions usually suffice:

1. **How long have you been working on this?** (Hours of deliberate practice — gives
   rough range.)
2. **Can you do <a representative beginner task> without looking it up?** (Distinguishes
   Aware from Novice; Novice from Amateur.)
3. **Can you teach someone else <a moderately complex aspect>?** (Distinguishes Amateur
   from Intermediate; Intermediate from Proficient.)

For broad domains, ask these per sub-skill.

## Level 0: Unaware

**Hours:** 0
**Description:** Completely unaware of the skill or topic. Hasn't encountered it
meaningfully and has no opinion on it.
**Example:** Someone who has never heard of the different types of clouds.

### Learning objectives

The key objective is to gain awareness of skills and sub-topics within an area of
interest:

1. **Cultivate Curiosity** — actively wonder about unfamiliar fields you encounter
   daily. Ask questions to spark interest.
2. **Engage with Casual Exploration** — read books, watch videos, or listen to podcasts
   that introduce new topics in an accessible way.
3. **Seek Simple Entry Points** — low-pressure ways to encounter new skills (tech
   news, beginner apps).

### Facilitation note

If the user is at Unaware, they probably aren't in a `/learn` session yet. They might
be exploring whether the topic is worth pursuing. Goal Setting hasn't started.

---

## Level 1: Aware

**Hours:** 0
**Description:** Recognizes the skill or topic but hasn't engaged beyond basic
awareness. May have heard about it or seen it referenced, but doesn't fully understand
it or feel motivated to explore.
**Example:** Someone who's heard of playing guitar but hasn't learned how it works.

### Level of intellectual understanding

Can identify the skill but can't explain or apply it yet.

### Challenges

- **Lack of Motivation** — without understanding relevance, hard to feel compelled to
  explore.
- **Uncertainty About Where to Start** — overwhelmed by available resources.
- **Perception of Difficulty** — the skill may seem too complex, creating a mental
  barrier.

### Learning objectives

Move from passive awareness to active curiosity. Lay the foundation for deliberate
practice.

1. **Simple Research** — explore introductory resources (articles, videos, tutorials).
2. **Identify Small Entry Points** — manageable ways to begin (a short blog, a simple
   task related to the skill).
3. **Set a Tiny Learning Goal** — 15 minutes to explore the skill further. Builds
   momentum without overwhelm.
4. **Join a Learning Community** — engage with people already practicing. Even passive
   participation can spark interest.
5. **Create a Curiosity List** — write down questions or aspects of the skill that
   intrigue you. A personalized roadmap for exploration.

### Facilitation note

This is often where Kickoff sessions begin. Don't push for full Goal Setting yet — set
a tiny learning goal first. Once they're a few hours in (Novice), revisit the goal.

---

## Level 2: Novice

**Hours:** 1–10
**Description:** Started practicing but in early stages. Some exposure, but
understanding and application are limited.
**Example:** Someone who has watched beginner programming tutorials but still needs
constant reference to examples.

### Level of intellectual understanding

Can recall basic terms and concepts but struggles to apply them. Often needs guidance
in new situations.

### Challenges

- **Information Overload** — overwhelming amount of new information; struggle to
  filter what's important.
- **Lack of Structure** — practice inconsistent, jumping between topics without
  mastering basics.
- **Difficulty Applying Knowledge** — even when theory is understood, struggle to
  apply in real-world contexts.
- **Impatience** — expecting fast results; frustration when progress feels slow.

### Learning objectives

Focus on absorbing information, even when it feels overwhelming. Comfort grows with
practice over time.

1. **Manage Cognitive Load** — focus on examples and repetition before attempting
   problem-solving. Internalize key concepts first.
2. **Balance Practice** — frequent practice over perfection. Comfort with basics
   matters more than accuracy at this level.
3. **Learn from Mistakes** — study common mistakes and how to avoid them.
4. **Master Fundamentals** — core concepts and techniques as foundation for advanced
   learning.
5. **Embrace Confusion** — don't shy away from concepts you don't fully understand.
   Early practice involves uncertainty; pushing through builds understanding.

### Facilitation note

This is the level where the agent's discipline matters most. **Don't introduce too many
chunks at once.** Don't push problem-solving before the user can recognize the patterns.
Hold back the temptation to teach more — the user needs *practice*, not more
information.

---

## Level 3: Amateur

**Hours:** 10–25
**Description:** More comfortable with basic concepts but still far from mastery.
Completed beginner tasks; can handle some intermediate challenges, though performance
is often inconsistent. May overestimate abilities.
**Example:** Someone who can play a few songs on guitar but struggles with transitions
or improvisation.

### Level of intellectual understanding

Can explain the basics, understand key concepts, see connections between parts. But
struggles when applying knowledge to new or complex situations.

### Challenges

- **Overconfidence** — may overestimate abilities; frustration when faced with more
  advanced tasks. (Dunning-Kruger first peak.)
- **Inconsistent Performance** — can complete basic tasks; complex problems expose
  gaps.
- **Difficulty with Complexity** — unfamiliar problems feel overwhelming due to lack
  of systematic approaches.
- **Frustration with Slow Progress** — initial rapid progress slows; can lead to
  impatience or self-doubt.

### Learning objectives

Develop consistency and refine foundational skills.

1. **Strengthen Foundational Skills** — revisit core concepts before more advanced
   topics.
2. **Structured Practice** — set specific goals for each session; focus on one
   sub-skill at a time.
3. **Seek Feedback** — actively from mentors, peers, or other resources.
4. **Consistency** — practice regularly to build muscle memory and good habits.
5. **Embrace Complexity** — start tackling more challenging problems to push and
   identify weak areas.
6. **Refine Problem-Solving** — break tasks into smaller steps, recognize patterns,
   understand *why* something works, not just *how*.

### Facilitation note

This is where the **Spar** mode becomes useful. Amateurs benefit from being challenged.
"You said you understand X — explain it to me without looking" surfaces gaps that
self-assessment misses. Use Devil's Advocacy lightly.

---

## Level 4: Intermediate

**Hours:** 25–100
**Description:** More confident; efficiently handles moderately complex tasks. May not
fully grasp how much more there is to learn.
**Example:** Someone who can write simple programs but struggles with intricate systems
or troubleshooting.

### Level of intellectual understanding

Can apply knowledge in familiar contexts, break down complex problems into parts, begin
to evaluate work. Creates original work but often relies on existing methods or
frameworks.

### Challenges

- **Overconfidence** — may overestimate abilities.
- **Inconsistent Performance** — familiar tasks easy; unfamiliar ones expose gaps.
- **Learning Plateau** — progress may slow; frustration or temptation to quit.
- **Impostor Syndrome** — increased awareness of what they don't know leads to
  self-doubt. (Dunning-Kruger first dip.)

### Learning objectives

Deepen understanding and refine practice. Bridge the gap between basic proficiency and
advanced mastery.

1. **Focus on Consistency** — practice similar tasks repeatedly to refine techniques
   and build consistency.
2. **Embrace Complexity** — tackle more challenging problems to push beyond comfort
   zones.
3. **Refine Problem-Solving** — systematic approaches.
4. **Solidify Basics** — revisit foundational knowledge to ensure mastery before more
   advanced topics.
5. **Expand Creativity** — original projects, creative problem-solving.

### Facilitation note

The Intermediate plateau is **structural**, not a sign of failure. The Dunning-Kruger
dip here is exactly when users tend to quit. Name it explicitly. Frustration at this
level often *means* the user is at the right edge of their ability.

---

## Level 5: Proficient

**Hours:** 100–500
**Description:** Confident and capable. Tasks once difficult are now manageable. Can
complete complex projects or handle advanced challenges with ease. Others may see them
as an expert; may be asked for advice or mentorship. May feel a plateau.

### Level of intellectual understanding

Apply knowledge consistently. Break down complex problems. Make judgments about own
work and others'. Create original solutions, but may still rely on established
frameworks.

### Challenges

- **Plateauing** — after rapid earlier progress, improvement feels slower.
- **Complacency** — tasks easier; potential lack of challenge.
- **Impostor Syndrome** — increased awareness of what's unknown leads to self-doubt.
- **Inconsistent Performance** — new or complex tasks can still expose gaps.

### Learning objectives

Achieve consistent performance while taking on more complex challenges. Break through
plateau and move toward mastery.

1. **Push Beyond Comfort Zones** — tackle more complex tasks, even uncomfortable ones.
2. **Structured Practice with Feedback** — targeted practice, seek feedback, work on
   specific weaknesses.
3. **Refine Problem-Solving** — systematic approaches; analyze deeply.
4. **Solidify the Basics** — revisit and reinforce foundational skills.
5. **Embrace Creativity** — apply knowledge creatively, experiment with new techniques.

### Facilitation note

Proficient users often resist working on weaknesses because most things feel easy.
Push for the uncomfortable specific weaknesses. Coach mode and Spar mode are both
useful. The plateau breaks when the user is willing to look bad at something again.

---

## Level 6: Competitive

**Hours:** 500–2000
**Description:** Proficient and striving for excellence in a competitive landscape.
Consistently above average. Praised for abilities, but may feel pressure to constantly
outperform — leading to perfectionism and burnout.

### Level of intellectual understanding

- **Analyzing** — breaking down complex problems and recognizing patterns
- **Evaluating** — making nuanced judgments about own and others' work
- **Creating** — innovating and synthesizing knowledge into new methods or solutions

### Challenges

- **Perfectionism** — striving for flawless work slows progress; leads to burnout.
- **Burnout** — pressure to continually outperform is exhausting.
- **Handling Failure** — setbacks feel personal; shake confidence.
- **Resistance to New Approaches** — sticking to proven methods limits growth.
- **Pressure to Maintain Excellence** — expectations to always excel create stress.

### Learning objectives

Maintain high performance while embracing complexity and innovation.

1. **Push Beyond Comfort Zones** — advanced challenges; unfamiliar tasks.
2. **Structured Practice with Feedback** — quality practice, target weaknesses, seek
   feedback.
3. **Systematic Problem-Solving** — break down tasks, analyze solutions for consistency
   across challenges.
4. **Embrace Humility and Keep Learning** — always more to learn; stay open to new
   techniques and feedback.
5. **Expand Creativity** — experiment, innovate, break through limits.

### Facilitation note

Burnout is a real risk at this level. Watch for signs — diminishing session frequency,
emotional flatness in journal entries, perfectionism creeping into chunk selection
("I have to master this one before moving on" when the actual answer is "do it
imperfectly and move on"). The agent should sometimes recommend rest.

---

## Level 7: Expert

**Hours:** 2000–10000
**Description:** Deep, specialized knowledge. Confident and capable. Often sought for
advice. Strong grasp of theory and practical application. Aware of how much more there
is to learn — balance of confidence and humility drives continued growth.

### Level of intellectual understanding

- **Analyzing** — complex problems, pattern recognition
- **Evaluating** — nuanced judgments
- **Creating** — innovating, synthesizing into new methods or solutions

### Challenges

- **Isolation from Peers** — fewer equals; can feel isolated.
- **Staying Humble** — overconfidence can creep in; harder to remain open to learning.
- **Balancing Innovation and Tradition** — established methods vs. new ideas.
- **Increased Scrutiny** — leaders face higher expectations.
- **Sustaining Motivation** — incremental progress harder to feel motivated by.

### Learning objectives

Deepen expertise, embrace innovation, contribute meaningfully to the field.

1. **Push Beyond Familiar Boundaries** — challenges outside immediate expertise.
2. **Structured Practice with Feedback** — peers at level or beyond; uncover blind
   spots.
3. **Contribute to the Field** — research, teaching, publishing new methods.
4. **Develop Strategic Vision** — beyond individual problems; shape the future of the
   field.
5. **Mentor and Guide Others** — teaching solidifies knowledge; offers fresh
   perspectives.

### Facilitation note

Experts rarely use `/learn` for the topic they're expert in. They might use it for
adjacent or new domains where they're at lower levels. Expert-level facilitation usually
means peer-level conversation: less scaffolding, more sparring, more references to
research and frontier work.

---

## How to use this reference

- **For Kickoff sessions** — light diagnostic in pre-flight; targeted use during Goal
  Setting Step 3 (target performance level).
- **For Session sessions** — usually unnecessary; the journal already records the level.
- **For Review sessions** — re-assess if the user has progressed a level (or
  regressed).
- **For Course-Correct sessions** — full re-assessment is the entry point. The user is
  often at a different level than they think.

When facilitating, **don't over-diagnose**. The level is a rough heuristic for matching
methods, not a label that needs precision.
