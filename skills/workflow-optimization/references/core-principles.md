# Core Principles of Workflow Optimization

These ten principles recur across every major workflow-improvement theory. Where
the six theory lenses each catch *specific* problems, these principles are the
*cross-cutting* check — apply them during the Sweep (Step 4) to catch issues that
sit between theories or above them.

Each principle below names the theories it draws from, so its pedigree is visible.

---

## 1. Define value from the customer's perspective

Value is whatever the *customer* of the workflow is willing to pay for, wait for,
or be satisfied by — not what the people inside the workflow assume is valuable.
Every step that doesn't contribute to customer-defined value is a candidate for
elimination.

*Drawn from:* Lean (specify value), Six Sigma (Critical-To-Quality / Voice of the
Customer), TQM ("quality is defined by the customer"), BPR (customer-centric
redesign).

**Check:** Who is the customer of this workflow? Have we asked them — or are we
assuming what they value?

## 2. Make the work visible before changing it

You cannot improve what you cannot see. Map, diagram, or model the workflow before
proposing changes. Visualization exposes hidden dependencies, shadow systems, and
rework loops that are invisible to the people running the process day to day.

*Drawn from:* every source — value-stream mapping (Lean), process mapping/SIPOC
(Six Sigma), the "panoramic" wall-sized process map (BPR), process mining (Process
Optimization).

**Check:** Do we have an accurate picture of the *actual* workflow — not the
idealized version people describe?

## 3. There is always a constraint — focus the vital few

Every system has at least one limiting factor. Effort spent improving anything
other than the constraint produces little or no gain. Likewise, a small number of
causes produce most of the problems (the Pareto 80/20 pattern). Concentrate effort;
do not boil the ocean.

*Drawn from:* Theory of Constraints (the constraint / weakest link), Six Sigma
(Pareto analysis — the vital few).

**Check:** What is the single biggest limiting factor here? Are proposed
improvements aimed at it, or at convenient but low-impact steps?

## 4. Eliminate non-value-added work — don't just automate it

The instinct to speed up a wasteful step with technology is a trap. Automating
waste produces faster waste. First ask whether the step should exist at all;
eliminate it if not; only then improve or automate what remains.

*Drawn from:* BPR (Hammer: "Don't automate, obliterate"), Lean (eliminate muda).

**Check:** For each step — would the customer miss it if it vanished? If not, why
are we keeping (or automating) it?

## 5. Decide with data, not assumption

Opinions about where a workflow hurts are frequently wrong. Measure: cycle time,
throughput, defect rate, wait time, volumes. Let evidence — not the loudest voice
or the oldest habit — drive the diagnosis.

*Drawn from:* Six Sigma (statistical, data-driven), Process Optimization
(operating data, process mining), TQM (systematic analysis).

**Check:** Is each claimed problem supported by a measurement, or is it a hunch?
What would we measure to confirm it?

## 6. Improvement is continuous — it is never "finished"

A workflow is never permanently optimized. Conditions change, and a fixed process
slowly decays. Build in a cycle of ongoing review rather than treating optimization
as a one-time project.

*Drawn from:* TQM and Six Sigma (PDCA / Plan-Do-Check-Act), Lean (kaizen), Theory
of Constraints (POOGI — process of ongoing improvement).

**Check:** Does the workflow have a built-in review cadence, or will it drift until
the next crisis?

## 7. Involve the people who do the work

The people performing the workflow know its real frictions, its undocumented
workarounds, and its shadow steps. Improvements designed without them tend to be
wrong, and tend to be resisted.

*Drawn from:* Lean (shop-floor involvement, "consult the people who do the work"),
TQM (organization-wide participation, quality circles).

**Check:** Have the actual workers been consulted, or is this a top-down redesign?

## 8. Optimize the whole, not the local part

Improving one step in isolation often *worsens* the system — it can just move the
bottleneck, or create inventory/queues elsewhere. Subordinate local decisions to
the performance of the end-to-end flow.

*Drawn from:* Theory of Constraints (subordinate everything to the constraint),
BPR (holistic, whole-process redesign), Lean (system-wide "smooth flow").

**Check:** Does this improvement help the whole workflow, or just one step at the
expense of another?

## 9. Reduce variation and standardize before optimizing

A process that behaves differently every time cannot be reliably improved —
you'd be optimizing a moving target. Standardize the work and reduce variation
first; then optimize the stable baseline.

*Drawn from:* Six Sigma (reduce variation, the central goal), Lean (standard work).

**Check:** Does this workflow run consistently, or does it vary by person, shift,
or day? Is there a documented standard?

## 10. Choose radical vs. incremental deliberately

Incremental improvement (kaizen) and radical redesign (re-engineering) are
different tools. A fundamentally outmoded workflow cannot be saved by small tweaks;
a basically sound one does not need to be demolished. Decide which fork you are on,
on purpose — based on how broken the workflow actually is.

*Drawn from:* BPR (radical redesign — "obliterate"), Lean/TQM (incremental kaizen).

**Check:** Is this workflow fundamentally outmoded (redesign it) or basically sound
but inefficient (improve it incrementally)? Have we chosen consciously?

---

## How to use these in the Sweep

After the six theory lenses, walk these ten principles as a final cross-cutting
pass. For each one, ask its **Check** question against the workflow. Anything that
surfaces here — and wasn't already caught by a theory lens — goes into the Lens
Ledger as a cross-cutting finding. This is the safety net under the safety net.
