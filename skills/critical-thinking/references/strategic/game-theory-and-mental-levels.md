# Game Theory & Mental Levels — the Structure-and-Depth Lens

*The three classics give you positioning, moves, and execution. They do not give
you the **structure** of an interaction — who moves, what each side gains, whether
both can win, what happens if it repeats — or a way to calibrate **how deeply your
opponent is reasoning**. That's this lens. It's the residual angle: the part of
strategy the classics leave open, and the one you reach for when the situation is a
genuine strategic interaction between thinking agents.*

This is a working distillation framed for use, not a course. Apply by asking the
detection questions against the real situation.

## When this lens does the most work

- There's a real, thinking opponent whose choice depends on *your* choice (and
  vice-versa) — not just a fixed obstacle.
- You're about to treat a fight as zero-sum, or about to over- or under-estimate
  how clever the other side is.
- The interaction repeats, or reputation carries forward.
- You need to coordinate, commit, threaten, or signal credibly.

## Principle 1 — Map the game before you play it

Every strategic interaction has a structure. Name it before reaching for a move:
the **players**, each side's **options**, the **payoffs** (what each actually gains
or loses), the **information** (who knows what), and the **sequence** (simultaneous,
or does someone move first?).

**Detection questions:**
- Who are the real players — including any silent third parties whose payoff matters?
- What does each side actually win or lose under each combination of moves?
- Does someone move first, and does moving first help or hurt here?
- What does each side know that the others don't?

## Principle 2 — Zero-sum or positive-sum? (the most expensive thing to get wrong)

In a zero-sum game your gain is their loss; in a positive-sum game both sides can
end up better off. **Treating a positive-sum game as a fight is one of the most
common and costly strategic errors** — you destroy value you could have shared. The
classics, born of war, bias toward zero-sum framing; correct for it here.

**Detection questions:**
- Is this actually win/lose, or could both sides come out ahead (a bigger pie)?
- What's the value-creating move you'd miss if you assumed it was a pure fight?
- Where are the genuinely zero-sum parts, and where are the cooperative parts? (Most
  real situations are mixed.)

## Principle 3 — Dominant moves, equilibria, and the cooperation trap

Look for a **dominant move** — one that's your best choice no matter what they do
(if it exists, it's usually the answer). Then ask where the game **settles** if both
sides play well: the stable point where neither wants to move unilaterally. Watch
for the **cooperation trap** (prisoner's-dilemma shape): each side acting in its own
interest produces a worse result for both — common in price wars, arms races, and
escalating feuds.

**Detection questions:**
- Do you have a move that's best regardless of their response? Do they?
- If both sides reason well, where does this land — and do you like that landing?
- Are you in a trap where mutual restraint beats mutual aggression, but neither
  dares go first? What would let you escape it (commitment, communication, repetition)?

## Principle 4 — Mental levels: aim one step above, not infinitely above

Players reason in levels: level-0 acts on instinct; level-1 best-responds to level-0;
level-2 anticipates level-1, and so on ("I think that you think that I think…").
The winning depth is **one level above your actual opponent** — no more. Reason too
many levels deep against a simple opponent and you out-think yourself into a move
they never set up; reason too shallow against a sophisticated one and you get
played. The skill is *calibration*: read their real depth, then sit exactly one
above it.

**Detection questions:**
- How many moves ahead is this opponent *actually* thinking — honestly, from
  evidence, not from fear or flattery?
- What's the move that beats their real level — without assuming they're a genius
  or a fool?
- Are you over-thinking a simple situation, or under-thinking a deep one?

## Principle 5 — Commitment, signaling, and credibility

Counter-intuitively, **limiting your own options can strengthen you** — a visible,
irreversible commitment (burning a bridge, a public stance, a binding contract)
changes the other side's calculation because they know you can't back down. But
threats and promises only work if they're **credible**; an empty threat is worse
than none. *(Signaling and bluffing sit on the ethics line — keep them to what
you're entitled to control; don't lie to someone who relies on you in good faith.)*

**Detection questions:**
- Is there a commitment you could make that changes their best response in your favor?
- Are your threats and promises credible — would you actually follow through, and do
  they believe you will?
- What are you signaling, and is it the signal you intend?

## Principle 6 — One-shot vs. repeated: the shadow of the future

A one-time interaction and a repeated one are different games. When you'll face the
other side again, **reputation and reciprocity become assets**: cooperative,
tit-for-tat play often beats exploitation, because today's move shapes tomorrow's
response. This is the structural reason the ethics guard's "deception tends to
backfire" point holds in repeated games — and why a one-shot win can be a long-run loss.

**Detection questions:**
- Will you deal with this party again? Does the relationship outlast this round?
- Does a win here cost you trust or reputation that's worth more over time?
- Could a cooperative opening invite cooperation back, changing the whole game?

## A note on coordination (focal points)

When you need to coordinate with someone without communicating, there's often an
"obvious" choice both gravitate to (a focal point) — the salient time, place, or
option. When the strategic problem is *aligning* rather than *beating*, look for the
focal point instead of out-maneuvering.
