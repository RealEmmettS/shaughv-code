# Feedback Types

Feedback is information about your performance or progress that helps identify mistakes
and misconceptions to improve. **Faster and more frequent feedback = faster
improvement.**

This reference is faithful to the user's Mental Toolbox source material. Used in the
Learning Loop's Phase 2 (Plan for Feedback).

## The principle

Practice without feedback is repetition. Practice with feedback is improvement. The
most common reason users plateau is that their feedback loop is missing, slow, or
unreliable. The agent's job during Phase 2 is to help them name the feedback they'll
get and from where.

---

## The Six Types of Feedback

### 1. Outcome Feedback

Tells you whether the result was successful or unsuccessful but **does not specify
what was wrong or how to improve.**

**Example:** Submitting code that fails a test — you know it didn't pass, but not why.

**Strengths:** unambiguous, often automatic.
**Weaknesses:** doesn't guide improvement.

**Best for:** later-stage practice where you can diagnose your own mistakes from a
pass/fail signal.

### 2. Informational Feedback

Highlights what was done incorrectly but **does not provide solutions.** Points out
gaps without guiding the learner on how to fix them.

**Example:** A grammar checker marking sentences as awkward without explaining why.

**Strengths:** more guidance than outcome feedback; surfaces specifics.
**Weaknesses:** still leaves the *how to improve* to the learner.

**Best for:** Amateur/Intermediate users who can interpret the gap and figure out the
fix.

### 3. Corrective Feedback

Provides guidance on what went wrong **and offers specific steps on how to improve.**
Most actionable. Best for beginners.

**Example:** A coach saying *"You're rotating your hips too late — start the rotation
when your front foot lands."*

**Strengths:** highest learning yield, especially early on.
**Weaknesses:** harder to find — usually requires a coach, mentor, or detailed answer
key.

**Best for:** Novice and Amateur users. Becomes harder to obtain as the user advances.

### 4. Internal Feedback

Stems from your own intuition and perspective. Self-assessment.

**Example:** Re-reading something you wrote and noticing it doesn't sound right.

**Strengths:** always available, free, fast.
**Weaknesses:** subject to your own blind spots — you can't see what you don't know.
Particularly unreliable at Novice and Amateur levels.

**Best for:** Proficient+ users with calibrated self-assessment. Risky at lower levels.

### 5. External Feedback

Feedback from sources outside yourself. Can be Outcome, Informational, or Corrective —
the type matters more than the source.

**Strengths:** corrects internal blind spots; brings in perspectives the user lacks.
**Weaknesses:** quality varies enormously. Some external feedback is highly subjective
and unhelpful (random opinions, low-context advice).

**Best for:** All levels. The trick is filtering for quality.

### 6. Output vs. Process Feedback

A meta-distinction:

- **Output Feedback** focuses on the final result.
- **Process Feedback** focuses on the steps, actions, and decisions made to reach the
  final product.

**Early on, Process Feedback from an expert is the most valuable form of feedback you
can get.**

**Example:** Output: "Your essay is unconvincing." Process: "You stated your thesis,
then jumped to evidence without bridging — you skipped the reasoning step."

The process tells you what to fix; the output only tells you that something is wrong.

## The Ten Sources of Feedback

### 1. Coaching

One of the best ways to receive Corrective Feedback. Also one of the most difficult to
obtain — depends on budget and niche.

**When best:** Beginning of a journey for any complex skill; breaking through a
plateau at Proficient+; for skills with non-obvious failure modes.

**Practical note:** Even one or two coaching sessions can save dozens of hours of
trial-and-error. Don't dismiss this as too expensive without doing the math on what
self-directed practice would cost in time.

### 2. Peer Feedback

Especially valuable in a workshop setting. **Asking for feedback on specific aspects**
improves the quality of what you receive — *"can you tell me whether the rhythm in
this section feels right?"* gets better feedback than *"thoughts?"*.

**When best:** Amateur and Intermediate users, where peers are at similar level and
can spot the same issues.

**Pitfall:** Peer feedback is often Outcome Feedback unless asked for specifics. Push
for Process Feedback by asking process-shaped questions.

### 3. Emulating Experts

Recreate what experts have created. Trying to reproduce reveals why certain aspects
work — and where your skills fall short.

**When best:** All levels, but especially Amateur+. Below Amateur, you don't have the
basics needed to recognize what makes the expert's version work.

**How:** Pick one specific work to emulate. Try to recreate it. Compare results. The
gap is the data.

### 4. Solving Worked Example Problems

Textbooks and online courses provide example problems with full step-by-step solutions.
Working them through and comparing your steps to theirs gives **process feedback** that
most problem-solving exercises can't provide.

**When best:** Problem-solving skills (math, programming, logic, debugging). Earlier
levels especially.

**How:** Read the problem. Work it yourself before looking at the solution. Compare
your steps to theirs at each stage. The divergence point is what you need to learn.

### 5. Teaching Others

Forces you to explain what you've learned and answer questions you may not have
encountered. The more people you teach, the more opportunities to learn new
perspectives.

**Even teaching yourself counts.** Writing an explanation as if to a real audience
recruits the same gap-finding mechanisms.

**When best:** Amateur+. Below that, you don't yet have the structure to teach.

**How:** Write a 3-paragraph explanation of the chunk to a hypothetical reader who
doesn't know the topic. Notice where you struggle to explain — those are your gaps.

### 6. Recording Yourself

Watching yourself practice gives an angle you can't see in real-time. Reveals
decisions and habits you didn't notice.

**Especially helpful for:** public speaking, music, sports, demos, presentations,
sales calls.

**Pitfall:** Watching yourself is uncomfortable. Many people record but don't watch.
Schedule the watching time explicitly.

### 7. Choosing Tools that Provide Feedback

Some tools have feedback built in; some don't. Choose tools that will tell you when
you're wrong.

**Examples:**
- **Code in a text file:** no feedback. **Code in an IDE with a debugger:** immediate
  feedback on syntax, runtime errors, type checking.
- **Music played without recording:** no feedback. **Music played into a tuner or
  recording app:** feedback on pitch and timing.

**When best:** All levels. The tool choice affects feedback quality more than most
people realize.

### 8. Self-quizzes

Test yourself; identify gaps in memory and understanding. Creating questions also
deepens understanding.

**Bonus technique:** Create questions while reviewing source material, then close the
book and **wait a few minutes** before answering. The delay turns this into active
recall.

**When best:** Subject-matter learning where retention matters. Pairs with spaced
repetition.

### 9. Active Recall Summary

Close the source material and **write a summary of everything you can remember.**
Without referencing the source, write what you know. Then review and compare to the
text — the gaps are what you missed or misunderstood.

**When best:** Every learning session, as part of the After Learning journal. See
`references/learning-journal.md`.

**Pitfall:** Skipping this when tired or rushed. The struggle is exactly what produces
the retention.

### 10. Self-reflection

Thinking about the subject or reviewing your notes. Different from active recall —
this is *processing* what you already have, looking for new perspectives or areas to
explore.

**When best:** Between sessions; during weekly reviews; when stuck.

**How:** Re-read the journal. Look for patterns: what keeps coming up? What's been
deferred but not addressed? What's the user feeling consistently?

## How to plan feedback for a learning session

In the Learning Loop's Phase 2, the agent walks the user through:

1. **What kind of feedback do you need?** (Outcome / Informational / Corrective;
   Output / Process)
2. **Which sources will provide it?** Pick 1–3 from the ten sources above.

For most sessions, two sources is plenty. More than three is overhead.

### Common pairings by skill type

| Skill type | Recommended pairing |
|---|---|
| Programming / problem-solving | Tools (IDE) + Worked Examples |
| Creative (writing, art, music) | Recording + Emulating Experts |
| Sport / muscle memory | Coaching (if available) + Recording |
| Subject knowledge | Self-quiz + Active Recall Summary |
| Public-facing skill (speaking, sales) | Recording + Peer Feedback |
| Long-term retention | Self-quiz + Spaced Repetition (Anki) |

## When the environment is wicked

In wicked environments — feedback unreliable, delayed, or misleading; patterns don't
repeat; rules change — natural feedback is too noisy to learn from. **Build artificial
feedback loops:**

- **Predictions before feedback** — write down what you expect *before* the result.
  The gap between prediction and reality is signal even when the reality itself is
  noisy.
- **Worked examples** — even when slow, they bypass the noise of the real environment.
- **Coaching** — wicked environments are where mentorship pays back the most. A coach
  who has navigated the same noise can extract signal you can't.
- **Decision journals** — record decisions, reasoning, and expected outcomes. Review
  later to detect patterns the noisy environment hid.

See SKILL.md's Wicked-Environment Guard for the full discipline.
