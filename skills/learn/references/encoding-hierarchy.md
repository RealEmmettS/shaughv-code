# Encoding Hierarchy

Six levels of encoding, from least effective to most. The brain conserves energy by
filtering out information it doesn't deem important. To retain learning long-term,
you have to **signal importance** through encoding and active recall.

This reference is faithful to the user's Mental Toolbox source material with minor
adaptation for the agent.

## Why this matters

Most users default to **structure-only** encoding — reading, listening, taking notes
without escalating. That's the lowest level of retention. Within hours, ~50% of new
material is forgotten; within a month, ~80%.

Each level of escalation up the hierarchy fights the forgetting curve more aggressively.
The cost is effort; the benefit is durability. The agent's job during the **Retain
phase** of the Learning Loop is to help the user pick the right level for the chunk.

## When to escalate

| Situation | Use this level |
|---|---|
| Chunk is one-off, won't recur | Structure |
| Need to remember in this session only | Chunking |
| Need to remember tomorrow | Sound + Chunking |
| Need to remember next week | Abstract or Tangible |
| Multi-month retention required | Tangible + Method of Loci |
| Critical professional knowledge | All applicable + Spaced Repetition |

---

## Level 1 — Structure

The lowest level of processing. We naturally remember the very beginning and very end
of a sequence (primacy and recency effects), but the middle gets lost.

### Example

Trying to remember the random 10-digit number `4259246168` by just looking at it.
Without strategy, you'll forget it within minutes.

### When this is enough

- One-time information you don't need to retain
- Material you can always re-look-up easily
- Skim-reading a survey article where the goal is awareness, not retention

### Why this fails for real learning

Most people think reading more carefully = retaining more. It doesn't. Re-reading is
one of the lowest-yield study techniques in the research. If retention matters,
escalate.

---

## Level 2 — Chunking

Break large pieces into smaller groups that fit working memory. Working memory holds
about 4±1 chunks. Chunking turns 10 items into 3 groups of 3 — which fits.

### Example

`7078737862` → `707-873-7862` (phone number format).

The number didn't get smaller, but each chunk became a single unit the brain can hold.
This is exactly why phone numbers are formatted with hyphens.

### How to use during a learning session

When a chunk on the syllabus has many sub-items:

1. Group the sub-items by some natural pattern (theme, source, sequence).
2. Name the group with a short label.
3. Remember the group name; the items within reconstruct from the label.

### Example for a learning session

Trying to remember the four critical thinking frameworks: instead of `Contemplating /
Decision-Making / Design / Problem-Solving`, chunk them as `the four CT frameworks`
plus a quick mnemonic to recover the list.

---

## Level 3 — Processing Sound

Engages a different part of the brain than visual structure. Saying it aloud, or even
sub-vocalizing, increases retention significantly.

### Example

`seven zero seven, eight seven three, seven eight six two` — said aloud, this is
notably easier to remember than the digits read silently.

### How to use during a learning session

- **Read new terminology aloud.** Say the names of new chunks; have the user say them.
- **Verbalize procedures.** Walk through a procedure by saying each step out loud.
- **Teach back verbally.** Even if no one is listening, explaining a concept *out loud*
  recruits more brain regions than silent thinking.

### When to recommend this

For terminology-heavy domains (medicine, law, languages), sound encoding is one of the
highest-yield moves available. The agent should explicitly suggest reading new terms
aloud for these topics.

---

## Level 4 — Abstract Concepts

Mnemonics that encode information as concepts or ideas. More demanding than sound or
structure, but lacks tangible sensory experience — so it's a step above sound but not
the most durable.

### Example

The number `7078737862` becomes:
- `707` → a Boeing 707 (or just "a plane")
- `873` → "Ate Stephen Tree"
- `7862` → "Stephen Ate Sticks Too"

Each chunk is now an abstract concept, easier to remember than digits.

### How to use during a learning session

- **Find a memorable association** for hard-to-encode chunks. The association doesn't
  have to be logical; it has to be sticky.
- **Use existing knowledge as anchors.** New material attached to an existing
  well-encoded concept inherits some of its retention.
- **Acronyms and initialisms** count here — `SMART` goals, `MECE` for mutually
  exclusive collectively exhaustive, etc.

### Pitfall

Abstract concepts are "flaky" — if the association isn't reinforced, it can fade. For
durable retention, escalate to Tangible.

---

## Level 5 — Tangible (Multi-sensory)

Engage multiple senses to make the abstract concept *physical* in the imagination.
Visual, auditory, kinesthetic, sometimes olfactory or gustatory. The richer the
sensory experience, the deeper the encoding.

### Example

The number `7078737862` becomes a mental story:

> *"That Boeing just ate Stephen, and some Trees, while Stephen Ate Sticks Too."*

Now imagine it: the 707 unhinging its jaw to gobble up Stephen and a tree, while
Stephen is simultaneously eating the sticks. **Absurd, vivid, multi-sensory.**

The absurdity helps. The brain remembers strange things better than ordinary ones.

### How to use during a learning session

- **For concepts:** ask the user to invent a vivid mental image for the concept. The
  weirder, the better.
- **For procedures:** physically act them out (or visualize doing so). Muscle memory
  + visual + auditory.
- **For facts:** invent a concrete, sensory scene that contains the fact.

### When to recommend this

For chunks where retention failure has been observed (i.e., the user keeps forgetting
this one). Tangible encoding is more effort, so reserve it for the chunks that actually
need it.

---

## Level 6 — Method of Loci (Mind Palace)

The strongest encoding technique. Uses the brain's natural ability to remember
**physical spaces** to anchor information. Also known as a Memory Palace.

### How it works

1. **Pick a physical space you know well** — your home, a route you walk, a building
   layout.
2. **Walk through it mentally** in a fixed order, identifying locations along the path.
3. **Place each chunk at a specific location**, using a vivid (Tangible-level) mental
   image.
4. **To recall:** mentally walk the path. Each location triggers the chunk you placed
   there.

### Example

Encoding `7078737862` with Method of Loci:

> *"I walk through my front door and find a Boeing toy (707) on the ground. After I
> pick it up, I keep walking to see my friends Tate(8) and Stephen(7) by a Christmas
> tree(3). I walk into my kitchen to find that Stephen(7) Ate(8) my Stick(6) Stew(2)!"*

The path through the house has fixed order, so the digits come back in sequence.

### How to use during a learning session

- **For long lists** (>7 items) that need durable retention: Method of Loci is the
  technique. Anything shorter usually doesn't justify the setup cost.
- **For multi-session retention** of a complex framework or system: build a palace
  for that domain that the user can return to across sessions.
- **For exam-style retention** where a large body of structured material needs to be
  available on demand.

### Pitfall

Building the palace takes time. For one-off material, simpler encoding wins on
effort/benefit. Reserve Loci for material the user needs to actually keep.

---

## Combining encoding with active recall

Encoding alone fades. **Pair encoding with active recall** — purposefully struggling to
remember without looking — to actually cement retention.

The combination:

1. **Encode at an appropriate level.**
2. **After 10–30 seconds of mental search**, attempt to recall.
3. **If you can recall correctly**, the encoding worked.
4. **If you can't**, escalate the encoding level and try again.

The 10–30-second struggle is what signals the brain that the information is important.
Re-reading without struggle does not produce that signal.

---

## Spaced repetition: the long game

Encoding + active recall at one moment in time isn't enough. The forgetting curve
drops to ~21% retention at one month without review.

**Spaced repetition** schedules reviews at increasing intervals to combat the curve:

| Review | Time after first encoding |
|---|---|
| 1st review | Within the same day |
| 2nd review | 1 day later |
| 3rd review | 3 days later |
| 4th review | 1 week later |
| 5th review | 2 weeks later |
| 6th review | 1 month later |
| 7th review | 2 months later |

Each successful recall extends the interval. Failed recalls shorten it.

### Anki

Anki is a flashcard tool that automates spaced repetition scheduling. Each card's
interval adjusts based on how easily the user recalls it. For domains with many
discrete chunks (vocabulary, terminology, anatomy, music theory), Anki is one of the
single highest-yield tools available.

### How to recommend Anki

- For Novice and Amateur users, Anki has a setup cost that often exceeds the benefit
  for early sessions. Wait until the user has clear chunks worth retaining (Amateur+).
- For language learning, medical/legal terminology, or vocabulary-heavy domains: Anki
  early.
- For procedures or skills: Anki is less useful than deliberate practice. Don't
  default to it.

---

## How to use this reference during a session

During the **Retain phase** of a Learning Loop session:

1. Identify the chunk(s) the user just practiced.
2. Ask: *"Do you need to remember this long-term, or is this one-time?"*
3. Match the encoding level to the answer. Don't over-encode one-time material; don't
   under-encode durable knowledge.
4. Pair with active recall: *"Without looking, what's the key thing you learned about
   <chunk>?"*
5. Update the journal's spaced-repetition schedule for chunks marked for long-term
   retention.

For long-running learning topics (multi-month), consider Method of Loci early. The
setup cost amortizes over the long retention period.
