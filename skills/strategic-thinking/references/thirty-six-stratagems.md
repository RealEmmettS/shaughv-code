# The 36 Stratagems — the Move-Finding Lens

*The thirty-six stratagems are a catalogue of indirect moves — ways to achieve an
aim when the direct path is blocked, too costly, or plays to the opponent's
strength. Used well, this lens is a generator of non-obvious options: it breaks the
fixation on "attack the problem head-on" and asks "what's the sideways move?"*

This file gives you both: the **canonical list of all thirty-six** (the source —
memorable four-character idioms, grouped in their six traditional chapters), and a
distillation into **six functional families** you can actually reason with, since
the canonical list is organized by battle situation rather than by what the move
*does*. The originals are an ancient public-domain compilation; the glosses and the
family synthesis below are written for use, not translated. Apply by asking the
detection questions — and read the **ethics line** before proposing any move that
relies on deceiving someone.

## When this lens does the most work

- The direct approach is blocked, or would mean hitting the opponent's strength.
- The user is stuck, out of obvious options, or feels they have to "just push harder."
- The user is weaker and needs leverage rather than force.
- After the Art of War lens has set the position, to find the actual *move*.

## The thirty-six, in full (the canonical list)

The traditional compilation groups the thirty-six into six chapters by the *state of
the contest* — from holding the advantage down to fighting from a losing position.
Glosses are brief and in plain terms; several lean on deception, and the ones marked
**[ethics]** routinely cross into manipulation — apply the ethics line before using
them.

**I. When you hold the advantage (Winning)**
1. *Cross the sea by deceiving the sky* — hide the real intent inside the perfectly ordinary and expected.
2. *Besiege Wei to rescue Zhao* — strike what they must defend rather than their strong point.
3. *Kill with a borrowed knife* — get a third party or their own resources to do the work. **[ethics]**
4. *Wait at ease for the exhausted foe* — let time and effort drain them; arrive rested.
5. *Loot a burning house* — press hardest when they're already in trouble.
6. *Clamor in the east, attack in the west* — misdirect attention away from the real target.

**II. When evenly matched (Confrontation)**
7. *Create something from nothing* — conjure a perception that shapes their behavior.
8. *Openly repair the walkway, secretly cross at Chencang* — an obvious decoy move masks the real route.
9. *Watch the fire from across the river* — let rivals exhaust each other; don't intervene too early.
10. *Hide a knife behind a smile* — lower their guard with warmth. **[ethics]**
11. *Let the plum die in place of the peach* — sacrifice the lesser to save the greater.
12. *Lead away the goat in passing* — take the easy gain the situation hands you.

**III. When you have the initiative (Attack)**
13. *Beat the grass to startle the snake* — probe to provoke a revealing reaction (and the inverse: don't tip off a hidden foe).
14. *Borrow a corpse to return the soul* — revive a dormant idea, banner, or vehicle for new ends.
15. *Lure the tiger down from the mountain* — separate the opponent from the ground or base that makes them strong.
16. *To catch something, first let it loose* — give slack now to take it more fully later.
17. *Toss out a brick to draw in jade* — bait with something minor to draw out something valuable.
18. *To catch the bandits, take their leader* — go for the linchpin, not the limbs.

**IV. In fluid, confused situations (Chaos)**
19. *Pull the firewood from under the pot* — attack the source of their power, not its symptoms.
20. *Muddy the water to catch the fish* — create or exploit confusion to act unseen.
21. *The golden cicada sheds its shell* — slip away leaving a convincing shell behind.
22. *Bolt the door to catch the thief* — seal the exits before you close in.
23. *Befriend the distant, attack the near* — ally with the far party to deal with the near threat.
24. *Borrow the road to take Guo* — turn access granted for one purpose into another. **[ethics]**

**V. Managing partners and subordinates (Proximate / Allied)**
25. *Swap the beams and pillars* — quietly replace the substance while the form looks unchanged. **[ethics]**
26. *Point at the mulberry to curse the locust* — correct or warn one party by aiming at another.
27. *Feign foolishness without losing balance* — play dumb while staying sharp.
28. *Lure them onto the roof, then take away the ladder* — commit them past the point of retreat. **[ethics]**
29. *Put flowers on a dead tree* — manufacture the appearance of strength.
30. *Turn the guest into the host* — convert a subordinate position into control, step by step.

**VI. When weak or cornered (Desperate / Losing)**
31. *The beauty trap* — exploit desire or distraction. **[ethics]**
32. *The empty fort* — project calm openness to bluff a stronger foe.
33. *Sow discord / the double agent* — turn their people against them or split their alliance. **[ethics]**
34. *The self-injury ploy* — accept apparent loss to win trust or sympathy. **[ethics]**
35. *The chain of stratagems* — link several so each one sets up the next.
36. *Retreat is the best move* — when the position is lost, withdraw intact to fight another day.

**How the canonical list maps to the six functional families below.** The chapters
above sort by *when* you're using a move; the families sort by *what the move does*,
which is more useful at the table. Roughly: Indirection (#2, 6, 8, 18) · Change the
ground (#15, 19, 23) · Patience (#4, 9, 16) · Trade the small for the large (#11, 17,
28) · Misdirection (#1, 7, 10, 29, 32) · The honorable exit (#36). The rest distribute
across these by situation.

## Family 1 — Indirection: attack what they must defend, not where they're strong

The classic "relieve the besieged by attacking the besieger's home." Don't engage
the opponent where they're braced for you. Threaten something they *have* to respond
to, and you pull them off their strong ground onto yours. Win the objective sideways.

**Detection questions:**
- What does the other side have to protect — that you could credibly threaten instead?
- Is there an adjacent objective that, if you take it, makes the contested one fall?
- Where are you pushing on the locked door when there's an open window beside it?

## Family 2 — Change the ground rather than win on it

Don't accept the contest as framed. Move it to terrain where you're strong: change
the venue, the rules, the timeframe, the terms, the audience, the basis of
comparison. "Lure the tiger off the mountain" — separate the opponent from the
advantage that makes them strong. "Remove the firewood from under the pot" — attack
the *source* of their power, not its symptoms.

**Detection questions:**
- What gives the other side their advantage here — and can you take that away rather
  than fight the advantage itself?
- Could you reframe the contest (different metric, different timeframe, different
  arena) so your strengths decide it?
- Are you fighting the symptom when the source is the real target?

## Family 3 — Patience: let the situation ripen

Sometimes the move is to wait — "sit on the hill and watch the tigers fight,"
"await the exhausted enemy at your ease." Let rivals weaken each other, let the
opponent overextend, let conditions turn in your favor before you spend anything.
Action isn't always strength; well-chosen inaction can be.

**Detection questions:**
- Is this a moment to act, or to wait while the situation moves in your favor?
- Are there other players whose conflict you could let play out?
- Is the opponent overextending in a way that time will punish without your help?

## Family 4 — Trade the small for the large

"Sacrifice the plum tree to save the peach." Give up something of lesser value to
secure the greater objective — a concession, a loss leader, a deliberate retreat on
a minor front. Strategy is allocation; the willingness to lose the right small
thing is what frees you to win the big one.

**Detection questions:**
- What are you clinging to that's small relative to the real objective?
- What concession would cost you little but buy you decisive position?
- Are you defending everything (and so defending nothing well)?

## Family 5 — Misdirection and the unexpected

"Make a feint to the east while attacking in the west." Do what the opponent
doesn't expect; show one intention and act on another; create a situation from
seemingly nothing to seize an opening. **This family overlaps heavily with the
ethics line — read it before using any of it.** The legitimate version is managing
*your own* signals and timing and being genuinely unpredictable. The illegitimate
version is lying to someone who relies on you. Stay on the right side.

**Detection questions:**
- What does the opponent expect you to do — and what becomes possible if you don't?
- What can you legitimately leave ambiguous to keep them preparing for the wrong thing?
- Is there an opening you could create by acting where no one's looking?

## Family 6 — The honorable exit is a strategy

The thirty-sixth and most famous: when the position is hopeless, withdrawing to
fight another day is the superior move, not the cowardly one. Preserving your
forces, your capital, your reputation, your optionality — so you're still in the
game later — often beats a doomed stand. Knowing when to fold is a strategic skill,
not a failure of nerve.

**Detection questions:**
- If this is unwinnable on current terms, what does a clean withdrawal preserve?
- Are you about to make a doomed stand out of pride or sunk cost?
- What's the move that keeps you in the larger game even if you lose this round?

## The ethics line (read before proposing any deceptive move)

Many of these stratagems — especially Families 5 and parts of 1 — can be run as
fair competitive positioning *or* as manipulation. The line:

- **Fair:** controlling your own signals, timing, and disclosures; being
  unpredictable; out-preparing and out-positioning; reframing a fair contest;
  shaping perception through what you legitimately choose to reveal.
- **Out of bounds:** lying to someone who relies on you in good faith; exploiting a
  vulnerability; deceiving where the context demands honesty (a fiduciary
  relationship, a safety matter, a vulnerable counterparty); anything illegal or
  that harms a third party.

When a move crosses the line, name it plainly and find a legitimate move that serves
the same objective. Two distinct reasons, kept separate: it's wrong (harming someone
who trusted you), and it's usually imprudent — in repeated games and relationships,
exposed deception burns the trust the next round depends on. The prudential point is
a strong tendency, not a law; weigh it for the situation rather than assuming it.

## Recording the findings

In the Lens Ledger, capture the candidate *moves* this lens generated — the
indirect angle, the ground-change, the wait, the trade, the unexpected play, or the
exit — and flag any that need the ethics line applied. This lens is meant to widen
the option set; don't narrow to one move yet (that's convergence).
